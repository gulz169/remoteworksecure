## The short answer

A phishing email is a message that pretends to be from someone you trust — your boss, your bank, a delivery company, or a tool you use every day — and tries to get you to click a link, open a file, or hand over a password. Remote workers are a popular target, because they work alone, they get many emails, and nobody is sitting next to them to double-check. The good news is that most phishing emails give themselves away. Check the sender's real email address, hover over links before you click, and treat any message that creates urgency — "your account will be closed today" — as suspicious until you have verified it through another channel.

## Why remote workers get targeted

When you work in an office, a strange email is easier to spot. You can walk over to a colleague and ask, "Did you send this?" When you work remotely, that check takes an extra step, and many people skip it. Attackers know this, and they design phishing emails around remote work itself: a fake message from IT asking you to reset your VPN password, a fake invoice from a supplier, a fake delivery notice for a parcel you are expecting, or a fake message from your manager asking you to buy gift cards.

Phishing is also cheap to send. One attacker can send thousands of emails in an afternoon, and only a small number of people need to click for the attack to work. The goal is usually one of three things: your password, your money, or access to your work computer.

## The warning signs

| Warning sign | What it looks like | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urgency and threats | "Your account will be closed in 24 hours" | Real companies rarely threaten you by email |
| Unexpected attachment | A file you did not ask for, often a .zip or .doc | Attachments can install malware |
| Link does not match the text | The text says "paypal.com" but the link goes elsewhere | Hovering over a link shows the real address |
| Wrong sender address | The name says "IT Support" but the address is a free email account | The display name is easy to fake |
| Generic greeting | "Dear customer" instead of your name | Real services usually use your name |
| Spelling and grammar mistakes | Odd phrasing, wrong capital letters, strange spacing | A sign the message was written quickly or translated |
| A request for money or gift cards | "Buy gift cards and send me the codes" | No real manager asks this by email |

## Check the sender first

The display name in an email is not the sender. Anyone can set their display name to "Microsoft Support" or "Your Manager". What matters is the email address behind it. On most email apps, you can tap or click the name to see the full address. Look at the domain — the part after the @ sign. A real email from your company comes from your company's domain. A real email from a bank comes from the bank's domain. If the address is a free email account, a misspelled version of a real company name, or a long string of random letters, treat the message as suspicious.

Also check the reply-to address. Some phishing emails come from a real-looking address but reply to a different one. If the reply-to address does not match the sender, do not reply.

## Check links before you click

Never click a link in a suspicious email. Instead, hover over it with your mouse, or press and hold it on your phone, to see where it really goes. The text of a link can say anything — "Log in to your account" — while the real address points somewhere completely different. Look for misspellings in the domain, extra words, or a domain that only looks like the real one, for example "micros0ft.com" with a zero instead of the letter o.

The safest habit is to not click at all. If an email says there is a problem with your account, open your browser, type the website address yourself, and log in there. If the problem is real, you will see it when you log in. If it is not there, the email was fake.

## Check attachments the same way

Attachments are the second most common way phishing works. A document or a zip file can install malware on your computer when you open it. Be suspicious of any attachment you did not expect, especially if the email creates urgency around it — "Open this invoice now or your order will be cancelled."

If you were expecting a file, check with the sender through another channel before you open it. A quick message on Slack or a phone call takes thirty seconds and can save you a much bigger problem. Never enable macros in a document from an email. Macros are a common way malware runs, and a real invoice or report does not need them.

## The gift card and payment scams

One of the most common phishing attacks aimed at remote workers is the gift card scam. You receive an email that looks like it is from your manager, asking you to buy gift cards for clients or for a team event, and to send the codes back by email. The email often says the manager is in a meeting and cannot be reached by phone. That is the point: the attacker does not want you to check.

No real manager asks an employee to buy gift cards by email. If you receive a message like this, contact your manager through a channel you trust — a phone call, a Slack message, or in person — and ask directly. The same rule applies to any unexpected request to send money, change a bank account, or pay an invoice urgently.

## What to do if you clicked

If you clicked a phishing link or opened a suspicious attachment, do not panic, but act quickly.

1. Disconnect from Wi-Fi or unplug your network cable. This stops malware from spreading or sending data.
2. Change your password from a different device, starting with your email account. Your email is the key to resetting everything else.
3. Turn on two-factor authentication if it is not already on. This stops an attacker from using a stolen password on its own.
4. Tell your employer's IT team, or your manager if you work for yourself. They need to know so they can check whether anything else was affected.
5. Run a scan with your antivirus software, and check your sent emails and bank statements for anything you do not recognise.

The worst thing you can do is hide it. A quick report lets your IT team fix the problem before it spreads. A hidden click can turn into a much bigger incident.

## A simple routine for every email

1. Stop and read. Who sent this, and was I expecting it?
2. Check the sender's real address, not just the display name.
3. Hover over links before you click, or do not click at all.
4. Treat urgency as a warning sign, not a reason to hurry.
5. When in doubt, verify through another channel — a phone call, a Slack message, or typing the website address yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a phishing email infect my computer if I only open it?

Opening an email is usually safe on its own. The danger is in clicking a link or opening an attachment. That said, some email apps load images automatically, which can tell the sender that your address is active. If you are unsure about a message, do not click anything in it, and delete it.

### What if the email looks exactly like a real one from my bank?

Attackers copy real emails carefully, including logos and layouts. That is why you should never trust the look of an email alone. Check the sender address, hover over the links, and if the message says there is a problem with your account, log in through the bank's own website or app — never through the link in the email.

### Should I report a phishing email?

Yes. Most email apps have a "Report phishing" button. Reporting helps your email provider block the same attack for other people. If the email pretends to be from your employer, forward it to your IT team as well, so they can warn the rest of the team.
