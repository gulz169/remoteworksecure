## The short answer

The 3-2-1 rule is the simplest backup system that actually works: keep 3 copies of your files, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 copy somewhere else entirely. For most remote workers, that means your laptop, a cloud service such as Google Drive or OneDrive, and an external hard drive or a second cloud service. If your laptop is stolen, your cloud copy saves you. If your cloud account is locked, your external drive saves you. If your home floods, the copy that is somewhere else saves you. Setting it up takes less than an hour, and after that it mostly runs by itself.

## What the 3-2-1 rule means

| Rule | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 copies | Keep your files in three places, not one | Laptop, cloud, external drive |
| 2 different types | Use two kinds of storage, not the same kind twice | Cloud service and a physical drive |
| 1 copy off-site | Keep one copy somewhere else entirely | Cloud, or a drive at another location |

The rule exists because every type of storage can fail. Laptops get stolen, dropped, or stop working. Cloud accounts get locked, or a service changes its terms. External drives wear out. The only defence is to never rely on one thing. Two copies are better than one, but if both copies are in the same house and the house floods, both are gone. That is why the off-site copy matters.

## What counts as a copy

A copy is only useful if it is complete and up to date. A folder you copied to a USB stick six months ago is not a backup — it is a snapshot of work you have already changed. A real backup is automatic, or at least regular, and it includes everything you would need to start working again: your documents, your spreadsheets, your photos, your browser bookmarks, and your password manager's export if it offers one.

Work files are the priority, but do not forget the rest. If your laptop dies, you will also want your email, your calendar, and your contacts. Those are usually stored in the cloud already, by your email provider, but check. If your email is only on your laptop, it is not backed up.

## Which tools to use

You do not need expensive software. Most remote workers can build a 3-2-1 system from tools they already have.

**Your laptop** is copy number one. It is where you work, so it is the original. Keep it organised — one main folder for work, with subfolders by project or client — so you know what needs backing up.

**A cloud service** is copy number two, and it is also your off-site copy. Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox, and iCloud all sync your files to the cloud automatically. If you use Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 for work, you may already have cloud storage included. Check how much space you have, and what happens when you run out. Cloud storage is usually charged per month, so check the provider website for current prices before you choose a plan.

**An external hard drive** is copy number three. A USB hard drive is cheap, fast, and does not need the internet. Plug it in once a week, copy your work folder to it, and unplug it again. Keeping it unplugged matters: a drive that is always connected can be damaged by the same power surge, ransomware, or theft that damages your laptop.

Some people use a second cloud service instead of a physical drive. That also works, and it is better if you travel a lot and do not want to carry a drive. The important thing is that the two cloud services are independent — if they are owned by the same company, they are not really two copies.

## How to set it up in under an hour

1. Make a list of what matters. Open your work folder and check what is in it. Add anything stored elsewhere — your desktop, your downloads folder, your documents folder.
2. Turn on cloud sync. Install the cloud service's app, choose your work folder to sync, and wait for the first upload to finish. The first upload is the slow one, because it sends everything. After that, only changes are uploaded.
3. Buy an external drive if you do not have one. Any USB drive with enough space for your work folder will do. You do not need the fastest or most expensive one.
4. Copy your work folder to the drive. Do it once by hand, so you know it works. Then set a weekly reminder — for example, every Friday afternoon — to plug the drive in and copy the folder again.
5. Test your backup. This is the step most people skip, and it is the most important one. Pick a file from your cloud copy and open it. Pick a file from your external drive and open it. If you cannot open them, your backup is not working, and it is better to find out now than when you need it.
6. Write down your system. One short note — "Work files are on my laptop, in Google Drive, and on the black USB drive in my desk drawer" — is enough. If you are ever away and someone else needs your files, they will know where to look.

## How often should you back up?

Cloud sync happens automatically, whenever you change a file, so that part takes care of itself. The external drive is the part you have to remember. Once a week is enough for most remote workers. If you work on something important every day — a client project, a book, a thesis — back it up every day, or at least copy that one folder to the drive at the end of each working day.

It is also worth checking your backup every month. Open a few files from each copy and make sure they open. Check that your cloud storage is not full. Check that your external drive still works. A backup you never test is a backup you cannot trust.

## The mistakes that lose people's work

- **Keeping only one copy.** A laptop is not a backup. If it is stolen or breaks, your work is gone.
- **Backing up to the same device.** A second folder on the same laptop does not help if the laptop is stolen.
- **Never testing the backup.** Many people discover their backup is broken only when they need it. Test it once a month.
- **Forgetting the off-site copy.** Two copies in the same house are not enough. A fire, a flood, or a burglary can take both.
- **Not backing up your password manager.** If you lose your password manager's data and you have no backup, you lose access to everything. Check whether your password manager offers an encrypted export, and store it with your backups.

## A simple weekly routine

- Monday: work as normal. Cloud sync handles itself.
- Friday afternoon: plug in your external drive, copy your work folder, unplug the drive, and put it back in its place.
- First Friday of the month: open a file from your cloud copy and a file from your drive, and check they open. Check your cloud storage is not full.

That is the whole system. It takes about five minutes a week, and it protects you from the most common ways remote workers lose their work.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cloud storage alone enough?

It is much better than nothing, and for many people it is the most important copy, because it is off-site and automatic. But it is not enough on its own. If your cloud account is locked, hacked, or you forget the password and cannot recover it, you lose access to everything. A second copy on an external drive protects you from that.

### How much cloud storage do I need?

Enough for your work files, with room to grow. Check how big your work folder is now, then choose a plan with at least twice that space. Prices vary by provider and by country, so check the provider website for current prices. If you are close to the limit, clean up old files or move large videos and photos to your external drive.

### What if I work on a company laptop?

Follow your employer's backup policy first. Many companies back up work files automatically through their own systems. If you are not sure, ask your IT team. You should not copy company files to a personal cloud account or a personal drive without permission, because that can break your employer's security rules.
