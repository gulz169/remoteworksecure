## The short answer

Two-factor authentication is the single most effective step you can take to protect your accounts. A password alone is not enough, because passwords get stolen in data breaches, guessed, or fished out of people by email. A second factor — usually a six-digit code from an app on your phone — stops an attacker who has your password but not your phone. For most remote workers, Google Authenticator or Microsoft Authenticator is the best choice: both are free, simple, and work with almost every service. Authy is a good option if you want your codes backed up in the cloud. 1Password is the best choice if you already use it as your password manager, because it can store your codes alongside your passwords.

This article is based on published information, not a hands-on review. We have not tested these products ourselves.

## Google Authenticator

Google Authenticator is the simplest authenticator app, and it is the one most services expect you to use. You scan a QR code when you set up two-factor authentication, and the app then shows a six-digit code that changes every thirty seconds. You type that code when you log in. It is free, it has no ads, and it works with almost every website and app that supports authenticator codes, not just Google services.

Google Authenticator now offers cloud backup with a Google account, so your codes are not lost if you lose your phone. That was its biggest weakness in the past, and it is now fixed.

Pros: free, simple, works almost everywhere, cloud backup available.
Cons: no desktop app, no extra features beyond codes.

## Microsoft Authenticator

Microsoft Authenticator works the same way as Google Authenticator, and it is also free. If you use Microsoft 365, Outlook, or Teams for work, it has one advantage: it can send you a push notification to approve a sign-in, instead of making you type a code. You just tap "Approve" on your phone. It also backs up your codes to your Microsoft account, so you can restore them on a new phone.

Pros: free, push approvals for Microsoft accounts, cloud backup, works with any service that supports authenticator codes.
Cons: push approvals only work with Microsoft accounts, not with every service.

## Authy

Authy is made by Twilio, and its main strength is backup. Your codes are encrypted and backed up to the cloud, and you can restore them on a new phone without setting up every account again. That makes it the best choice if you change phones often, or if you have lost your codes before. Authy also has a desktop app, so you can get codes on your computer as well as your phone.

One thing to know: Authy stopped offering new desktop downloads in recent years, based on its published announcements, so check its website for the current desktop situation before you rely on it.

Pros: free, encrypted cloud backup, easy to restore on a new phone, desktop app.
Cons: fewer updates in recent years, check the current status on the provider website.

## 1Password (built-in authenticator)

If you already pay for 1Password, you do not need a separate authenticator app. 1Password can store your two-factor codes alongside your passwords, and it fills them in automatically when you log in. That is more convenient than opening a second app, and it means your codes are backed up in your 1Password vault.

There is a trade-off. If someone gets into your 1Password account, they get your passwords and your codes together. That is why your 1Password master password must be strong, and why you should turn on two-factor authentication for 1Password itself, using a separate app or a hardware key.

Pros: codes and passwords in one place, automatic filling, backed up in your vault.
Cons: paid, codes and passwords in the same place if your vault is compromised.

## YubiKey (hardware key)

A YubiKey is a small physical key that plugs into a USB port or taps against your phone. Instead of typing a code, you touch the key to approve a sign-in. It is the strongest form of two-factor authentication, because there is no code to steal by phishing — an attacker would need the physical key in their hand. It is also the least convenient, because you have to carry it, and you should buy a spare in case you lose the first one.

For most remote workers, an authenticator app is enough. A YubiKey is worth considering if you handle sensitive data, manage money, or have been targeted before.

Pros: strongest protection, cannot be phished, no codes to type.
Cons: costs money, you must carry it, losing it without a spare is a problem.

## Which authenticator should you use?

- Choose Google Authenticator if you want a free, simple app that works everywhere.
- Choose Microsoft Authenticator if you use Microsoft 365 or Teams for work.
- Choose Authy if you change phones often and want easy cloud backup.
- Choose 1Password if you already pay for it and want codes with your passwords.
- Choose a YubiKey if you want the strongest protection and do not mind carrying a key.

## How to set up two-factor authentication

1. Pick an authenticator app and install it on your phone.
2. Go to the security settings of an account — start with your email, because it is the key to resetting everything else.
3. Turn on two-factor authentication and choose "authenticator app" as the method.
4. Scan the QR code with your app, and type the code it shows to confirm.
5. Save the backup codes the service gives you, somewhere safe and offline — for example, printed on paper in a drawer.
6. Repeat for your bank, your password manager, and your work tools.

Set it up on one account at a time. It takes about two minutes per account, and once it is done, logging in only takes a few seconds longer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if I lose my phone?

This is the most common fear, and it is why backup matters. If your app backs up to the cloud, install it on your new phone and sign in to restore your codes. If it does not, use the backup codes you saved when you set up two-factor authentication. That is why saving those codes matters. If you have neither, you will need to contact each service and prove who you are, which takes time.

### Is SMS two-factor authentication good enough?

SMS is better than nothing, but it is weaker than an authenticator app. SMS codes can be intercepted if someone tricks your phone company into moving your number to their SIM card. An authenticator app generates codes on your phone, so there is nothing to intercept. If a service offers both, choose the app.

### Do I need two-factor authentication on every account?

Start with the accounts that matter most: your email, your bank, your password manager, and your work tools. Those are the accounts an attacker wants. Once those are protected, add it to the rest over time. Every account you protect makes the rest safer too.
