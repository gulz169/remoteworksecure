## The short answer

Remote work security is not one big thing, it is a list of small things done properly. Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi, store your passwords in a password manager, turn on two-factor authentication for every important account, keep your software updated, and back up your work regularly. None of these steps is hard on its own, and together they protect you from the most common problems remote workers face. This checklist covers all of them, in the order you should do them.

This article is based on published information, not a hands-on review. We have not tested these products ourselves.

## The checklist at a glance

| Area | What to do | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VPN | Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi, kill switch on | Every time you work in public |
| Password manager | Store every password, use unique passwords | Set up once, use daily |
| Two-factor authentication | Turn on for email, bank, work tools | Set up once, check yearly |
| Updates | Install operating system and app updates | Monthly, or when prompted |
| Backups | Back up your work to the cloud and an external drive | Weekly, automatic if possible |
| Router | Strong admin password, WPA3 or WPA2, firmware updated | Set up once, check monthly |
| Devices | Screen lock on, find-my-device turned on | Set up once |
| Phishing | Check the sender before you click a link | Every email |

## 1. VPN

A VPN encrypts your traffic on networks you do not control, such as cafe Wi-Fi, hotel Wi-Fi and co-working spaces. Turn on your VPN before you open email or work tools, and turn on the kill switch so your traffic is blocked if the VPN drops. Set the VPN to start automatically when your laptop starts, so you do not have to remember it. If your employer provides a work VPN, use that for work traffic, and ask your IT team before you install a second one.

## 2. Password manager

A password manager stores all of your passwords in one encrypted vault, so you only need to remember one master password. Use it to create a unique password for every account, because reusing the same password across accounts means one breach gives an attacker access to everything. Share logins with clients or colleagues through the password manager, never by email or chat, and remove access as soon as a project ends. Turn on two-factor authentication for the password manager itself.

## 3. Two-factor authentication

Two-factor authentication, also called 2FA, adds a second step when you log in, usually a code on your phone. Even if someone steals your password, they still need the code. Turn on 2FA for your email first, because your email can be used to reset most of your other accounts. Then turn it on for your bank, your password manager, your cloud storage, and your work tools such as Slack and your project management app. Use an authenticator app rather than SMS when you have the choice, because SMS codes can be intercepted.

## 4. Keep your software updated

Install updates for your operating system, your browser and your apps. Updates often fix security problems that attackers use, and an old version of a browser or an app is an easy target. Turn on automatic updates where you can, and check once a month for updates that did not install automatically. This includes your router firmware, which is easy to forget.

## 5. Back up your work

Back up your work in two places: the cloud, and an external drive. Cloud storage such as Google Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive protects you if your laptop is stolen or damaged. An external drive protects you if your cloud account is locked or compromised. Set backups to run automatically, so you do not have to remember them. Try restoring a file once every few months, because a backup you have never tried to restore is not a backup you can trust.

## 6. Secure your home network

Your home router is the front door of your home office. Change the admin password from the default, update the firmware, and turn on WPA3 or WPA2 encryption with a strong Wi-Fi password. Turn on the guest network for visitors and for smart home devices, so they cannot see your work laptop. Turn off remote administration, so no one can manage your router from the internet. Check the list of connected devices once a month and look for anything you do not recognise.

## 7. Lock your devices

Set a screen lock on your laptop and your phone, with a short timeout, so they lock themselves when you walk away. Turn on find-my-device on both, so you can locate, lock or erase a lost device. Encrypt your laptop hard drive, which is usually a single setting in Windows or Mac, so that if your laptop is stolen, no one can read your files without your password.

## 8. Watch out for phishing

Phishing is still the most common way remote workers are attacked. A phishing email looks like it comes from your bank, your employer or a client, and it asks you to click a link or download a file. Before you click, check the sender address carefully, and look for spelling mistakes or urgent language such as "your account will be closed today". If an email asks you to log in, go to the website yourself instead of clicking the link. If you are not sure whether an email is real, ask the sender through a different channel, such as a phone call or a message in your work chat.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the single most important step on this checklist?

Turn on two-factor authentication for your email. Your email can be used to reset most of your other accounts, so protecting it protects everything else. If you do nothing else on this list, do that first.

### Do I need all of this if I only work from home?

Most of it, yes. A VPN matters less at home, but a password manager, two-factor authentication, updates and backups matter just as much at home as they do in a cafe. Phishing does not care where you work.

### What should I do if I think my account has been hacked?

Change the password immediately, from a device you trust, and turn on two-factor authentication if it is not already on. Check your account for changes, such as a new email address or a new device, and undo them. If it is a work account, tell your IT team or your employer straight away.
